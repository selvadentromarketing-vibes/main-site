Mostly yes, with real caveats. Tulum is a major international tourist destination and carries the crime profile of one: petty theft, scams, and occasional nightlife-associated incidents that travel far in headlines. Day-to-day resident life is generally calm. Where you go, when you go, and how you handle valuables matter more than the town's reputation.

I handle sales at Selvadentro, which means I have an obvious interest in you feeling good about Tulum. So this page does the opposite of reassurance: it tells you what actually happens, what residents do about it, and where a gated community helps and where it does not.

## What kinds of incidents actually happen in Tulum?

Three categories, in descending order of how likely they are to touch you.

**Opportunistic theft.** Phones and bags lifted at beach clubs, valuables taken from parked cars and unlocked rentals, scooters and bicycles stolen. This is the overwhelming majority of what residents and visitors experience, and almost all of it is preventable.

**Money and commercial scams.** Taxi overcharging, ATM machines that skim or push terrible exchange rates, restaurant bills with items you did not order, rental deposits paid to someone who does not own the property, and — in real estate specifically — land sold without clean title. That last one costs more than every other item on this page combined; we cover it in [why informal land deals go wrong](/en/guide/ejido-land-mexico-risks).

**Nightlife-associated violence.** Tulum has had incidents connected to the party economy — disputes over drug distribution around clubs and beach venues, occasionally with bystanders affected. These are the events that generate international coverage, and they are the reason Tulum's reputation swings so far from the lived experience. They are geographically and behaviorally concentrated: late, in specific venues, in specific corridors.

I am deliberately not publishing crime statistics. Numbers for a municipality this size move fast, get quoted out of context, and are frequently misreported. If you want data, go to the primary source and read the current figures yourself rather than trusting anyone's summary — mine included.

## Why does Tulum's reputation swing so hard?

Because two Tulums exist in the same municipality. There is a nightlife and hospitality economy along the beach road and in the club corridor, and there is a residential town where people take kids to school, shop at the supermarket, go to the gym, and work. An incident in the first one is international news. The second one produces no news at all, which is exactly why it is invisible in your search results.

Neither picture is a lie. They are just different neighborhoods, at different hours, with different populations.

## Does daily resident life feel different from the headlines?

For most residents, yes — noticeably. The routine texture of living here is closer to a growing beach town than to anything dramatic: traffic, construction, humidity complaints, cash-only businesses, slow paperwork. People walk their dogs. Cafés are full of laptops.

What experienced residents do differently from visitors is mostly boring. They do not carry much cash, they do not go to unfamiliar clubs at 3 a.m., they know which stretches of road are poorly lit, and they treat their car as unsecured storage. The adjustment is real but small, and it looks a lot like what you would do in any large city.

## What are the practical precautions?

1. **Choose your zones consciously.** The character of Tulum changes street by street. Spend time in an area at night before you commit to living there.
2. **Avoid late-night nightlife you cannot vouch for.** Almost every headline-grade incident is tied to that specific scene at that specific hour.
3. **Do not leave anything in a parked car.** Not in the trunk, not under a seat. This is the single most common loss.
4. **Use ATMs inside bank branches**, during the day, and refuse the machine's offer to charge you in your home currency. Standalone tourist ATMs are where card skimming and terrible rates live.
5. **Carry small amounts of cash and one card.** Leave passports, extra cards and jewelry in a safe.
6. **Arrange rides you can trace.** Use the app options available, a hotel- or community-arranged driver, or agree the fare before you get in. Never negotiate mid-ride.
7. **Watch your card at all times.** Pay at the terminal, in front of you, in pesos.
8. **Never pay a property deposit or land payment to a personal account** against a private contract. Payments go against notarized instruments — full stop.
9. **If you are stopped while driving,** stay calm, keep documents in order, and ask for a written citation rather than settling roadside. This is standard resident advice, not an accusation.
10. **Build a small local network** — neighbors, an administrator, a doctor, a mechanic. Local knowledge is the real security system.

## Should I check a travel advisory?

Yes, and check it yourself rather than reading someone's characterization of it. The United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and most European governments publish country- and state-level travel advice for Mexico, updated on their own schedules and written for their own citizens. Quintana Roo is usually treated differently from Mexico as a whole.

I am not quoting a level here on purpose: advisories change, and a number frozen into a marketing page is worse than no number. Read the current text from your own government, note what specifically it warns about, and weigh it against what you plan to actually do here.

## What do gated communities with controlled access really change?

They change the perimeter, the eyes and the maintenance — not the country you are in. Concretely:

| | What it genuinely changes | What it does not change |
|---|---|---|
| Controlled access | Only known vehicles and registered visitors enter; there is a record | Nothing about the roads you drive outside |
| 24/7 security | Someone is awake, patrolling, and reachable at 3 a.m. | It is a deterrent and a response, not a guarantee |
| Single entry point | Opportunistic theft becomes impractical — no casual foot traffic | Anything you leave in town, in your car, or at a beach club |
| Neighbors and low density | Empty houses get noticed; absent owners have someone checking | Requires an HOA that actually funds it |
| Lighting and upkeep | Maintained roads and lit streets, funded by the HOA fee | Your own habits, which still do most of the work |
| Vacation absence | The main practical benefit for non-resident owners | Insurance, which you should still carry |

That is the honest version of why gated communities are the default for residential development on this coast — including ours. It is not that danger is everywhere. It is that a controlled perimeter removes the easiest category of loss and makes leaving a property empty for three months a reasonable thing to do. ## So — would I tell a friend to move here?

Yes, with the same sentence I have used all page: choose your zone, keep your night-time judgment, treat valuables like they are visible, and do your legal homework before any payment. People who do those four things generally describe Tulum as calm. People who ignore them generally have a story.
