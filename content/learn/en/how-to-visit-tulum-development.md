Visit twice if you can, and treat the visit as an inspection rather than a tour. See the actual lot and its access road, ideally in the rain, walk to the amenity locations and cenotes, look at neighboring construction, then verify everything off site with an independent notario. A full checklist follows.

I run sales, which means I am the person who normally designs these visits. So here is the version I would want as a buyer — including the parts that are inconvenient for a salesperson, because a visit that only shows you what we chose to show you is not diligence.

## What should you actually see on site?

**Your specific lot, staked and walked.** Not "a lot like yours." Walk the boundaries, look at the ground, note the slope, the rock, the trees you would keep and the ones you would need permission to touch. A lot on a plan and a lot under your feet are different objects.

**The access road, in the rain if the weather offers it.** This is the highest-value ten minutes of any visit in this region. Jungle access roads in dry season are a fair-weather promise; the same road after an afternoon storm tells you what construction logistics and daily life will actually be like. If you cannot see it wet, ask what the surface is, how drainage was designed, and who maintains it.

**Where the amenities will physically be,** not where they are rendered. Stand at the clubhouse location. Measure the walk from your lot in minutes. Amenities you have to drive to are a different lifestyle than amenities you walk to.

**The cenotes.** Get in one if you are allowed. This is the asset that cannot be replicated, and it is also where you learn how seriously a developer treats the aquifer — access design, whether the perimeter is respected, whether there are structures where there should not be. Ours are documented on [the cenotes page](/en/cenotes), but see them.

**Neighboring construction, inside and outside the fence.** Inside tells you what your future neighbors are building and whether the rules are enforced. Outside tells you what the surrounding land is zoned to become. A quiet jungle lot with an unbuilt commercial parcel behind it is a different bet than you think.

**Infrastructure that exists rather than infrastructure that is planned.** Ask to see installed water lines, electrical distribution, the street, the access gate. Built infrastructure is a delivery record.

## What should you ask while standing there?

Ask on site, out loud, and write down the answers with the name of who gave them.

Ask for the **expediente** — the permit file — by reference, and whether you may photograph it. Ask what the **delivery obligations** are for your lot and for the amenities, and what happens if those dates slip; the clause-level version is in the guide on [what happens if the developer doesn't deliver](/en/guide/developer-fails-to-deliver). Ask for the **HOA rules and the community regime** as documents. Ask **who maintains what** and what the fee covers. Ask **what is sold** in the phase you are looking at and what is left. And ask the question that separates confident developers from nervous ones: *may I take all of this to my own notario?*

## The site-visit checklist

1. **Book the visit yourself,** and decline the offer to be picked up if it means you never drive the roads.
2. **Drive the route from the airport and from town.** Time it. Drive times sell projects; verify the ones you were quoted, including at rush hour.
3. **Walk your specific lot,** boundaries staked, and photograph each corner.
4. **Drive the access road,** wet if possible, and photograph the worst section.
5. **Stand at each amenity location** and time the walk from your lot.
6. **Visit the cenotes** and note the access design and the protected perimeter.
7. **Photograph installed infrastructure:** water, power, street, gate, security post.
8. **Look at neighboring construction** inside the community and at the land immediately outside it.
9. **Ask for the expediente** and note its reference numbers.
10. **Collect documents, not promises:** regime, HOA rules, building rules, contract draft, price and payment schedule in writing.
11. **Record names and titles** of everyone who answers a question.
12. **Return at night or at dusk** if you can, or drive the nearest town after dark. Places feel different when the sales day ends.
13. **Talk to an existing owner** — ask to be introduced, then ask them what surprised them.
14. **Leave without signing anything.** Any pressure applied in the next 48 hours is itself a data point.

That last item matters more than the other thirteen. "Today-only" pricing is a recognized pattern, not a favor.

## What has to happen off site?

Three things, all of them independent of the developer.

**Your own notario.** Not the one recommended in the sales office. Have them pull the property record at the Registro Público de la Propiedad and confirm the title path, the owner of record, and any liens or encumbrances. This is the step that catches the problems a visit cannot see.

**A permit check you run yourself.** Verify the expediente against state and municipal records, and check whether the project has appeared in any SEDETUS bulletin — including, in our case, the September 2025 review, where Selvadentro was listed on the 11th and removed on the 16th after proving compliance. The [step-by-step verification method](/en/blog/verify-tulum-development-legal-sedetus) covers exactly how to run these checks on any development, ours included.

**Local reality-testing.** Walk the nearest town at night. Buy groceries. Look at what a supermarket run, a school run or a clinic visit would involve. Sit somewhere with a coffee and ask two people who live here what they think of the area. None of that appears in a brochure and all of it appears in your life.

## How do you run a remote visit?

Sometimes flying in is not realistic before a decision. A remote visit can be genuinely rigorous if you insist on control of the camera.

Ask for a **live video walk**, not an edited film, and direct it: "walk to the boundary marker," "point the camera down the access road," "show me the water line." Ask for **raw dated photos** rather than renders. Ask for the **document set by email** and read it with your notario before the call, so your questions are specific. Use a **360° virtual tour** — ours exists — to orient yourself on the masterplan, then use the live call for the things a tour cannot answer. Have your notario or a local advisor **visit on your behalf**, which is normal practice and worth its cost. And verify [the location and drive times](/en/location) against a map yourself rather than accepting a graphic.

## How many visits does this take?

One visit is enough to decide a place is not for you. Two is usually the right number before signing: the first to see whether the project is real, the second — ideally in different weather, with your questions sharpened and your notario briefed — to confirm it.
