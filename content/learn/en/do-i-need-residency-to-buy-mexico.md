No. You can buy property in Mexico as a tourist, on a standard visitor permit, with no residency and no visa beyond the one that got you through the airport. Residency governs how long you may stay in the country, not whether you may own — and owning property does not, by itself, grant residency.

This confusion costs people months. Buyers postpone a purchase while they chase a visa they do not need for the purchase, or they assume a deed will fix their immigration status. The two systems are separate. Below is what each one actually controls.

## So what do I actually need to buy?

A valid passport, your immigration document — the visitor permit stamped or issued on entry is sufficient — and the funds. In the restricted zone, which includes all of Tulum because it sits within 50 km of the coast, a foreign buyer also needs a [fideicomiso](/en/glossary/fideicomiso): the bank trust that holds legal title while you hold every right of ownership. The notario constitutes it at closing.

That is the whole list. The notario público will ask for identification and tax data, not for a residency card. Our [full foreign-buyer walkthrough](/en/blog/buying-land-tulum-foreigner-fideicomiso) covers the closing sequence step by step.

## What does residency actually change?

Not your right to own. What it changes is everything around living here.

| | Visitor (tourist) | Temporary resident | Permanent resident |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can buy property | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Fideicomiso needed in restricted zone | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Length of stay | Short stays, renewed by leaving and re-entering | Multi-year, renewable | Indefinite |
| Household goods import (menaje de casa) | No | Yes, within a defined window | Yes, within a defined window |
| Foreign-plated vehicle | Temporary import permitted | Generally permitted | Generally not |
| Opening a Mexican bank account | Usually difficult | Usually straightforward | Straightforward |
| Getting an RFC (tax ID) | Possible, and often needed anyway | Simpler | Simpler |
| Mexican mortgage access | Very limited | Limited but real | Better, still selective |
| Work in Mexico | No | With authorization | Yes |
| Public health system enrollment | No | Possible | Possible |

Read that table as the practical answer to "should I get residency?" — if you plan to live here, furnish a house from abroad, bank locally or work, residency is worth the paperwork. If you are buying land to hold or to build on later while visiting a few times a year, you may genuinely not need it.

## How do temporary and permanent residency work, roughly?

Broadly, Mexico offers two tracks. **Temporary residency** is granted for a first year and renewed for additional years up to a defined maximum, after which you may apply to convert to permanent. **Permanent residency** is indefinite, with no renewals.

Both are usually applied for by economic solvency — proven income over recent months, or investments and bank balances over a longer period — and both have alternative routes through family ties to a Mexican citizen or resident, or through investment in a Mexican company. Retirees frequently qualify on pension income.

Here is the part where you should distrust every blog post, this one included: the qualifying thresholds are tied to Mexican minimum-wage and UMA references, they are updated, and consulates interpret and document them differently. Anyone quoting you an exact dollar figure is quoting a number that may already be stale. Both applications normally begin at a Mexican consulate abroad, not inside Mexico, and are then finalized at an INM office after you arrive. So: check the current requirements with the Mexican consulate nearest you, and treat that conversation as the only authoritative one.

## Does owning property get me residency?

No — not on its own. Property ownership is not an immigration category in Mexico. What it can do is support a solvency case: real estate held in Mexico is an asset, and assets form part of the financial picture a consulate reviews. Useful evidence, not a qualifying route.

The mirror-image question comes up just as often: does buying without residency put your property at risk? It does not. A registered escritura or a fideicomiso stands on its own regardless of how long your last visitor permit ran. Your title does not expire when your stay does.

## Does residency change my taxes?

Yes, and this is the section worth paying a professional for. Two examples, framed as typical rather than promised.

**On rental income**, anyone earning rent in Mexico owes Mexican income tax (ISR) and generally needs an RFC to comply properly — residency or not. Residency mainly makes the administrative side easier. The detail sits in the guide on [renting out a Tulum property](/en/guide/renting-out-tulum-property).

**On selling**, tax residency matters a great deal. Non-residents are commonly taxed by withholding on the gross sale price, or alternatively on the net gain at a higher rate where they qualify to elect it. Residents may access primary-residence exemptions, but those are conditional and evidence-heavy — they generally require the property to be your actual home, with documentation to prove it. Note the vocabulary trap: *tax* residency and *immigration* residency are related but not the same test.

If a sale is anywhere in your plans, talk to a cross-border accountant before you buy, not before you sell. The structure you close with is difficult to change later.

## Do I need an RFC or a bank account?

An RFC — Mexico's tax ID — is not required simply to buy. You will want one if you rent the property out, if you want deductible expenses recognized, or to smooth the tax side of an eventual sale. It is obtainable as a non-resident, though residency makes it easier.

A Mexican bank account is convenient rather than necessary. Many owners pay HOA fees and services by international transfer for years. If you want local banking, temporary residency is usually the practical unlock.

## Which should you actually pursue?

Match the document to the life, not to the purchase. Buying land and visiting seasonally works fine on visitor status. Living here half the year, importing your furniture, banking locally or building a house you will supervise in person points to temporary residency. Settling permanently points to permanent.

What you should not do is delay a purchase decision over an immigration question that does not gate it.
