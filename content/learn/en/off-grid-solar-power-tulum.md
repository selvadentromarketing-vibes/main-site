Usually yes. The Yucatán gets strong sun year-round, which makes solar productive here. The real question is not whether to install panels but which configuration: grid-tied, hybrid with batteries, or fully off-grid. The honest driver of that decision is air conditioning, and the honest constraint is canopy shade.

I sell land in a jungle community, so I have a commercial interest in the romance of energy independence. I would rather give you the version that survives a first electricity bill. Solar here is genuinely good and genuinely worth doing; going fully off-grid on a whim is how people end up with an expensive battery bank and a generator they resent.

## How much sun does Tulum actually get?

A lot, and — more importantly for design — consistently. This latitude has no dark winter: production dips during the rainy months from roughly June through October, when afternoon cloud and storms cut output, then recovers. There is no season where a well-sized array stops being useful.

Two local realities shape real-world yield. Heat reduces panel efficiency slightly, so the hottest hours are not automatically the most productive. And humidity plus salt-carrying air means mounting hardware and connectors should be specified for a marine-adjacent tropical environment, not for a dry inland one. Panels also need washing more often than owners expect; dust, pollen and organic debris from a living canopy accumulate fast.

## Grid-tied, hybrid or off-grid?

| | Grid-tied | Hybrid (grid + batteries) | Full off-grid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | Lowest | Moderate to high | Highest |
| Battery bank | None | Sized for essentials | Sized for everything, plus reserve |
| Complexity | Low — panels, inverter, meter | Medium — storage and switching logic | High — full design discipline |
| Behavior in an outage | Stops producing, by design | Keeps chosen circuits running | Unaffected, if sized correctly |
| Resilience in storm season | Depends entirely on the grid | Strong | Strongest |
| Ongoing attention | Minimal | Some monitoring | Real ownership: you are the utility |
| Backup generator | Rarely needed | Optional | Usually still wanted |
| Best for | Most homes with a reliable connection | Homes that want comfort through outages | Remote lots, or a deliberate choice |
| The catch | You are still a CFE customer | Batteries are the cost and the wear item | A/C makes this genuinely expensive |

The pattern we see most among people building well in this region is hybrid: a solar array sized against real consumption, plus enough storage to carry the essentials — refrigeration, water pump, internet, lights, one or two bedroom air conditioners — through an outage, while the grid covers peak load. It buys most of the resilience for a fraction of full independence's cost.

## Why does air conditioning decide the system size?

Because in this climate everything else is a rounding error. Lights, laptops, a fridge and a water pump are a modest load. Cooling a house through a humid May night is a different category of demand entirely, and it is the number that turns a manageable system into a large one — especially off-grid, where every cooling hour has to be stored, not just generated.

Which means the cheapest energy decision you will make is architectural, taken before any equipment is quoted:

1. **Orient and shade the building** so the west face is not absorbing afternoon sun.
2. **Design for cross-ventilation** — the regional vernacular did this for centuries without electricity.
3. **Insulate the roof properly.** In the tropics the roof is the enemy, not the walls.
4. **Choose inverter mini-splits** and cool the rooms you occupy rather than the whole house.
5. **Add ceiling fans everywhere.** Air movement buys several degrees of perceived comfort for almost no power.
6. **Manage humidity, not just temperature.** Dry air at 26 °C feels better than damp air at 23 °C, and costs less.
7. **Then size the system** against the load you actually engineered.

Design decisions here interact with the building rules you inherit — COS 35%, CUS 70%, two levels plus a roof deck at Selvadentro — so read [how COS and CUS shape a house](/en/blog/building-rules-tulum-cos-cus) before your architect draws a roof you cannot cover in panels.

## What about the trees?

This is the real jungle-specific constraint, and it is a good problem to have. Preserved canopy shades roofs, and shade is exactly what a panel does not want. Partial shading hurts disproportionately: one shaded panel can drag down a whole string unless the system is designed for it.

There are clean answers. Use microinverters or per-panel optimizers so shaded modules do not penalize the rest. Put the array where the canopy already opens — a roof deck, a carport, a pergola over a terrace — rather than clearing trees for it. And accept a slightly larger array in exchange for keeping the canopy, which is, after all, the reason to build here rather than in a cleared subdivision. The same logic underpins [low-density development](/en/blog/low-density-development-tulum): the trees are the product.

Clearing mature canopy to improve solar yield is a bad trade in a community whose value comes from the jungle staying up. It is also usually not permitted.

## Can I sell surplus power back to CFE?

In general terms, yes: CFE offers interconnection contracts for small-scale distributed generation, and the standard arrangements credit the energy you export against what you consume rather than paying cash for every kilowatt-hour. There are capacity thresholds, meter requirements and paperwork.

The specifics change, so treat this as a category rather than a quote and work with an installer who has filed these contracts recently in Quintana Roo. Ask to see a client's post-installation bill; that single document tells you more than any brochure.

## What about outages and storm season?

Grid interruptions happen, and they cluster in storm season from June through November. This is where hybrid systems earn their cost — not in the monthly bill, but on the third day after a storm when your water pump still works.

Two honest notes. Grid-tied systems without storage shut down during an outage as a safety requirement, so panels alone do not make you outage-proof. And mounting matters: hardware should be specified and installed for local wind loads, because a poorly anchored array is a liability in a hurricane rather than an asset.

## What does the community provide?

Selvadentro's infrastructure includes sustainable energy and an underground water network across the community, along with controlled access, 24/7 security and internet in common areas. Buried distribution is more storm-resilient than overhead lines and keeps the canopy intact — the two reasons it costs more to build.

What that leaves to you is your own house: the array, the storage and the design decisions above, within the community's building rules. The two systems are related — [your water setup](/en/guide/drinking-water-tulum) depends on a pump, and a pump depends on power — so plan them together rather than in sequence.
