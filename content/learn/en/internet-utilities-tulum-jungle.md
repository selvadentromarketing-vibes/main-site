Yes, with deliberate setup. Fiber is common in Tulum town — typically 50 to 150 Mbps — while jungle and rural lots more often rely on satellite internet such as Starlink, or on fixed wireless. Grid outages happen, so a battery inverter or solar backup is normal. Tap water is not potable anywhere in Mexico.

I run Selvadentro, so I have skin in this answer. Here is the version I would want if I were the buyer: what you can actually get, what breaks, and what it costs you in planning rather than in money.

## What internet can you actually get on a jungle lot?

Three realistic options, and most serious remote workers here end up with two of them.

**Satellite (Starlink)** is the default outside the fiber footprint. It works, it is fast enough for video calls, and it can be installed anywhere you can get a clear view of the sky — which is the catch on a jungle lot. Dense canopy blocks it. The practical answer is placing the dish above the canopy line, on a roof or a mast, or in a genuine clearing. Heavy tropical downpours cause brief degradation; a fifteen-minute storm can interrupt a call.

**Fixed wireless** from local providers means a small antenna on your roof aimed at a tower. Where line of sight exists it is stable and cheaper than satellite. Where it does not, it is not an option at all, so this one is site-specific — ask neighbors, not salespeople.

**Cellular data** as a backup. A dedicated router with a Mexican SIM, or tethering from a phone, covers a satellite outage. Telcel generally has the broadest rural coverage in Mexico, which is why most residents carry it whether or not it is their main line.

The honest planning rule: if your income depends on being online, budget for two independent connections and a small battery. That is the same advice you would get for a rural property anywhere, and it costs less than one lost week of work.

## Is the fiber in Tulum town reliable?

Broadly yes, and it has improved substantially as the town has grown. Speeds in the 50 to 150 Mbps range are typical, coworking spaces run on it, and the digital-nomad economy here would not exist otherwise. The caveats are ordinary: installation can take weeks, service interruptions happen during storms, and coverage stops where the town's built-up grid stops — which is precisely why jungle-side lots are a different conversation.

## How reliable is the power grid?

The national utility, CFE, supplies the region, and service is generally continuous. But outages happen — during storms, during the rainy season, and occasionally with no explanation — and they can last from minutes to a day or more after a serious weather event. Voltage fluctuation is the quieter problem: it kills electronics slowly.

What residents actually do: surge protection on the whole house, a voltage regulator for sensitive equipment, a small inverter-plus-battery so the router, laptop and a few lights survive an outage, and increasingly solar with battery storage — which on this latitude makes obvious sense and reduces the sting of a grid failure to nothing.

## Can you drink the tap water?

No — nowhere in Mexico, including Tulum, and including new construction. This is not a Tulum problem or a jungle problem; it is a national norm, and every household here uses either 20-litre bottles or a home filtration system. We wrote a separate page on the options because it is the single most common practical question we get: [drinking water in Tulum](/en/guide/drinking-water-tulum).

## Is there cell coverage in the jungle?

Along the main roads and around the developed communities on the cenote route, yes. Deep in uncleared jungle, coverage becomes patchy — normal for rural Mexico. Two practical notes: check signal standing on the actual lot, not at the entrance gate, and consider a dual-SIM phone so you are not dependent on one carrier's coverage map.

## Why does wastewater deserve more attention here than elsewhere?

Because of what is under the ground. The Yucatán Peninsula is porous limestone with an underground river system running through it — the same system that fills the cenotes people swim in. There is no thick clay layer filtering mistakes. Whatever leaves a poorly built septic system reaches the aquifer faster here than in almost any other geology.

That is why proper treatment is a substantive question and not a formality, and why it is worth asking any development how it handles wastewater before you ask about the clubhouse. ## What does a work-from-jungle setup look like?

1. **Two connections.** Primary satellite or fixed wireless, secondary cellular, with automatic failover if your router supports it.
2. **Mount the dish above the canopy.** Survey the sky view before you finalize the roof design, not after.
3. **Battery-backed router and workstation.** A modest UPS or inverter covers the majority of outages entirely.
4. **A real office with cross-ventilation and a door.** Humidity and heat make a laptop-on-the-terrace fantasy last about a week.
5. **Surge protection everywhere**, plus a voltage regulator for the expensive equipment.
6. **Water storage** — a cistern with several days of capacity, plus your filtration choice.
7. **Confirm coverage and services on the specific lot** in writing, not on the development's brochure.
8. **Have a fallback.** Coworking in town, or a café you trust, for the day something genuinely fails.
