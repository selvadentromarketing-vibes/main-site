No — tap water is not potable anywhere in Mexico, Tulum included, and every household here either buys it or filters it. The water itself comes from an underground karst aquifer: the same flooded cave network that feeds the cenotes. That connection is the reason wastewater handling matters more here than almost anywhere else.

This is one of the first practical questions new residents ask and one of the last things sales material addresses. It deserves a straight answer, because the geology behind it also explains the building rules you will live under.

## Why isn't the tap water drinkable?

Not because the water leaving a well is necessarily bad, but because the delivery chain cannot be guaranteed. Distribution networks across Mexico carry pressure loss, older pipe, and storage in rooftop tanks and cisterns where contamination can enter after treatment. The result is water that is fine for washing and unreliable for drinking.

So the national habit is universal rather than regional: Mexicans and foreigners alike drink bottled or filtered water at home, in restaurants and in offices. Nobody in Tulum treats this as a hardship; it is simply how the kitchen works.

## What do households actually do?

Three approaches, usually combined.

| | Garrafones (20 L jugs) | Under-sink filtration | Whole-house system |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it does | Delivered drinking water | Purifies water at one tap | Treats everything entering the house |
| Upfront cost | Almost none | Low to moderate | Highest |
| Ongoing cost | Per jug, delivered weekly | Filter replacements | Filters, media, occasional service |
| Effort | Lifting and swapping jugs | Change filters on schedule | Scheduled maintenance |
| Handles hard water | No | Only at that tap | Yes — the real reason people install one |
| Best for | Renters, short stays, backup | Most households, most of the time | Owners building a permanent home |

Two notes worth having before you spend money. Regional groundwater is hard — high in dissolved limestone — which scales kettles, shower glass, fixtures and appliances. A softener or conditioner is often the upgrade people appreciate most, and it has nothing to do with drinking water. And whatever you install, keep garrafones as backup: pumps fail, power cuts happen, and a case of drinking water in the pantry solves both.

## Where does the water come from?

Under the Yucatán Peninsula there is no meaningful surface river system. What exists instead is karst: porous limestone dissolved over millennia into an enormous network of caves, underground rivers and pools, holding fresh groundwater that floats above denser saltwater intruding from the coast.

Cenotes are simply the places where the roof of that system collapsed and opened it to the sky. So a cenote is not a pond that happens to sit on a property — it is a window into the region's drinking water. Nine mapped cenotes sit inside our project, which is the single most concrete way to understand what "the aquifer runs under everything here" means. If the geology interests you, [what a cenote actually is](/en/blog/what-is-a-cenote-tulum) covers the formation in detail, and [the nine cenotes inside Selvadentro](/en/cenotes) are documented individually.

## Why does that make wastewater the real issue?

Because porous limestone does not filter. In a clay or granite landscape, contamination moves slowly and gets absorbed. Here it travels through open conduits, quickly, and arrives in the same water people swim in and pump.

That single fact explains most of the region's environmental debate and most of its serious building rules. It is why septic practice, treatment capacity and drainage design deserve harder questions than the amenity list. It is why low ground coverage matters: at Selvadentro the limits are COS 35% and CUS 70%, meaning at least 65% of every lot stays unbuilt, so rainwater keeps infiltrating through soil and root systems instead of sheeting off concrete. Those numbers, worked through on real lot sizes, are in [the guide to COS and CUS](/en/blog/building-rules-tulum-cos-cus).

So the questions to ask any development, ours included: where does the water come from, how is it distributed, what happens to wastewater, and how far is any treatment or disposal point from the nearest cenote?

## What does water infrastructure look like in a jungle community?

Two paths exist. Individual: each lot drills or shares a well, with its own pump, cistern and treatment — cheaper to start, entirely your problem forever. Or communal: the development builds a network, and homes connect to it.

Selvadentro's infrastructure includes an underground water network across the community, alongside sustainable energy, controlled access and 24/7 security. Underground matters for reasons beyond appearance: buried lines survive storms better than overhead alternatives, and they leave the jungle canopy intact — the [65% of preserved jungle](/en/eco-friendly-developments-tulum) only stays preserved if the infrastructure is designed around the trees rather than through them.

If you are comparing communities, ask to see the water and drainage plan, not a rendering of it. A development that can show you the network can usually show you its permits too.

## What should I set up in a new house?

1. **Cistern with adequate capacity.** Storage decouples you from outages and pressure drops. Size it for your household plus a few days.
2. **Pump plus pressure system,** and a plan for what happens when power is out — which connects directly to how you handle energy.
3. **Sediment pre-filter** at the entry point. Cheap, and it protects everything downstream.
4. **Water softener or conditioner** if you care about fixtures, appliances and glass. Hard water is the region's quiet maintenance tax.
5. **Point-of-use purification** at the kitchen tap: reverse osmosis or a rated multi-stage filter for anything you drink or cook with.
6. **Garrafón backup**, always. Two jugs in the pantry is the cheapest insurance in the house.
7. **Wastewater done properly** — sized correctly, sited away from the aquifer's vulnerable points, connected to the community system where one exists.
8. **A maintenance calendar,** because filters that are never changed are worse than no filters. Set reminders on the day you install them.
9. **Rainwater capture,** optional but sensible: the rainy season delivers a lot of free water for gardens and cleaning.

## Can I shower and brush my teeth with it?

Yes. Showering, washing, laundry and dishes with tap water are entirely normal here, and most residents brush their teeth with it without incident — though visitors with sensitive stomachs often use bottled water for the first week or two, which is a reasonable precaution rather than a rule. Ice and drinking water are where you should be consistent: filtered or bottled, at home and when you can choose.

None of this is exotic once you have done it for a month. It is a cistern, a filter and a standing delivery order.
