Tulum sits inside the Caribbean hurricane belt and the season runs June through November, so no property here is immune. Exposure varies sharply by site: beachfront faces storm surge, erosion and salt, while inland jungle land faces wind, rain and fallen trees without surge. Reinforced concrete construction and property insurance are the regional norm.

I run Selvadentro, so read the final section knowing that. Everything before it is the answer I give anyone weighing coastal Mexico against the jungle side — including people who end up buying somewhere else.

## When is hurricane season, and does Tulum actually get hit?

The Atlantic hurricane season officially runs June 1 to November 30, and activity concentrates in the later months. Tulum is on the Caribbean coast of the Yucatán Peninsula, which puts it inside the belt rather than beside it.

The regional record is public and worth reading before you buy anything here. Wilma crossed Quintana Roo in October 2005. Beryl came ashore on the Quintana Roo coast near Tulum on July 5, 2024. Both sit in the U.S. National Hurricane Center's storm archive, alongside every other Atlantic system on record; Mexico's Servicio Meteorológico Nacional (CONAGUA) tracks the same storms locally, and state Protección Civil issues the alerts residents actually act on.

Most seasons pass without a landfall near Tulum. Some do not. The honest framing is a low-frequency, high-consequence event you plan for — not a risk anyone should wave away, and not a reason to avoid the Caribbean either.

## What is the real difference between beachfront and inland exposure?

Storm surge. That one mechanism separates the two risk profiles more than anything else. Surge is the wall of water a hurricane pushes ashore, and it is what destroys ground floors, undermines foundations and reshapes beaches. It reaches the coast. It does not reach several kilometres inland.

| Risk | Beachfront property | Inland jungle property |
|---|---|---|
| Storm surge / coastal flooding | Primary structural threat | Not a factor |
| Beach erosion, shoreline loss | Real and permanent; land itself can shrink | Not a factor |
| Sustained wind | Full exposure, no windbreak | Real; canopy absorbs some, adds debris |
| Heavy rain, ponding | Significant | Significant — drainage design matters |
| Falling trees and debris | Palms, structures, sand | The main damage source: mature trees, branches |
| Salt corrosion (ongoing, not just storms) | Constant — hardware, glass, A/C, rebar | Minimal |
| Power and internet outages | Days, occasionally longer | Days, occasionally longer |
| Blocked access roads | Coastal road closures | Fallen canopy across access roads |
| Insurance cost | Highest in the region | Lower, still priced for wind and water |
| Post-storm cleanup | Sand, water intrusion, shoreline works | Vegetation, debris, drainage clearing |

Read that table as a shift in the type of problem, not a promise that one side is safe. Inland trades a catastrophic, water-driven risk for a disruptive, vegetation-and-wind-driven one.

## What still goes wrong on an inland jungle lot?

Wind does not stop at the beach road. A strong system inland means sustained wind loads on the roof and openings, wind-driven rain finding every unsealed joint, and days without grid power or internet. Mature jungle is the specific inland variable: it shades your house beautifully and it drops limbs. Trees too close to the roofline, dead wood left in the canopy and unmanaged growth over an access road are the difference between a week of cleanup and a repaired roof.

Rain is the other underrated one. A single storm can drop the kind of volume that finds any flaw in site drainage, so grading, roof runoff and how water leaves your lot deserve real attention at design stage.

## How does the Riviera Maya build for this?

The regional standard is reinforced concrete: concrete block or poured walls, cast-in-place slabs, steel reinforcement tied through. It is why the peninsula's building stock generally comes through storms intact while lightweight roofs and palapa structures do not. Beyond the shell, the specifics that matter are hurricane shutters or protective panels for every opening, glass rated for impact where budget allows, properly anchored roof elements, and generous drainage.

Community rules feed into this too. Where a development caps coverage and height — in our case COS 35% and a maximum of two levels plus a roof deck — the built profile stays low and the canopy stays dense. That is not a shield. It is one fewer variable, plus a landscape that recovers rather than a cleared plain.

## Can you insure a property in Mexico against hurricanes?

Yes, and it is routine. Mexican insurers sell homeowner policies covering hydrometeorological risk, and mortgage-free foreign owners buy them the same way locals do. Two things to understand: hurricane claims typically carry a deductible expressed as a percentage of insured value rather than a flat amount, and coverage terms deserve reading in the season before you need them, not during a watch.

While you hold land and have not built, there is very little to insure — the exposure arrives with the house. That is one quiet advantage of buying land and building deliberately: you get to specify the storm-resistant details from the start instead of inheriting somebody else's shortcuts.

## What should you have ready before a storm?

1. **Review insurance in May**, before the season opens. Confirm what is covered, the deductible, and how a claim is filed from abroad if you are not resident.
2. **Install shutters or panels** for every opening, and test that they actually fit and can be closed by two people in an hour.
3. **Manage the canopy pre-season.** Remove dead wood, trim limbs over the roof and the access route. This is the single highest-value inland habit.
4. **Keep drinking water and non-perishable food** for several days. Tap water is not potable anywhere in Mexico regardless of the weather — see our [guide to drinking water in Tulum](/en/guide/drinking-water-tulum).
5. **Plan for no power.** Fuel, charged battery banks, a generator or inverter, and lights that do not depend on the grid.
6. **Digitize your documents** — escritura or fideicomiso, insurance policy, IDs — and keep originals off the floor and sealed.
7. **Know your channels.** Follow state Protección Civil and the Servicio Meteorológico Nacional, not social media rumor, and know the nearest shelter.
8. **Name who checks the property** if you are abroad: property manager, neighbor, or the community's own security team.

## Where does Selvadentro sit on this map?

Plainly: Selvadentro is inland jungle, on Tulum's cenote-route side, roughly twenty minutes from the hotel zone. Storm surge is not part of our exposure profile, and salt corrosion is a marginal issue rather than a maintenance line item. Wind, rain, outages and fallen trees absolutely are, which is why 65% preserved canopy comes with canopy management, why the water network runs underground, and why the HOA carries a reserve for long-term maintenance. That is an exposure profile, not immunity — nobody on this coast gets to sell immunity.
