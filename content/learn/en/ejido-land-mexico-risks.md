Ejido land is communal agrarian property held by a community, not by an individual owner — and while it remains ejidal it legally cannot be sold, titled, or held by a foreigner at all. Buying it through an informal "cesión de derechos" is the single most common way buyers lose money on Mexican land. The only safe version is land converted to full private title (dominio pleno) and registered — which you can verify yourself.

I run Selvadentro, and this page exists because the ejido question deserves a direct answer before anyone compares developments — ours included. The rule is simple: private, registered title or no deal.

## What exactly is an ejido?

An ejido is a form of communal land tenure created after the Mexican Revolution: land granted to a community, worked by its members (ejidatarios), governed by an assembly (asamblea ejidal), and registered at the Registro Agrario Nacional (RAN) — not at the regular property registry. Ejidatarios hold *use rights*, not private ownership. Large parts of the land around Tulum — like much of rural Mexico — have ejidal origins, which is exactly why the question matters here.

## Why is buying ejido land so dangerous?

Because what is being sold is not ownership. The typical informal deal is a **cesión de derechos** — a "transfer of rights" document, sometimes even notarized-looking, that transfers, at best, one person's possession rights. It creates none of the protections a real purchase does:

| | Private land (escritura) | Ejido land (informal deal) |
|---|---|---|
| What you get | Registered title (or fideicomiso for foreigners) | A paper about possession "rights" |
| Registered at | Registro Público de la Propiedad (RPPC) | Nothing, or only agrarian records |
| Can a foreigner hold it? | Yes, via fideicomiso in the coastal zone | **Never** |
| Double-sale protection | Yes — registration is public | No — the same "rights" can be sold twice |
| Bank financing / insurance | Possible | No |
| If it goes wrong | Courts + title system | Little practical recourse |

Two failure modes repeat constantly. First, **double sales**: with nothing registered, nothing stops a seller from "ceding" the same parcel to several buyers. Second, **assembly reversals**: rights that were never properly individualized can be contested by the ejido community itself, years later. Litigating either from abroad is slow, uncertain and usually not worth the land.

## Can ejido land ever become legal to buy?

Yes — and this is the nuance sellers exploit. Through a formal process, the asamblea ejidal can individualize parcels and adopt **dominio pleno** (full private ownership). Once that conversion is complete and the parcel is registered at the RPPC as private property, it is ordinary land: it can be sold, escriturated before a notario, and held by a foreigner through a fideicomiso. The scam version skips the conversion and sells you the *promise* of it. Until dominio pleno is registered, you are not buying property — you are buying someone's paperwork problem.

## How do I check any lot in 10 minutes?

1. **Ask the direct question:** "¿El terreno es propiedad privada con escritura, o de origen ejidal? ¿Está inscrito en el Registro Público?" A serious seller answers with documents, not stories.
2. **Ask for the folio real** — the parcel's ID at the Registro Público de la Propiedad — and have a notario of your choosing pull the record: current owner, liens, history.
3. **If anything mentions ejido**, ask for the dominio pleno resolution and its RPPC registration. No registration, no purchase — whatever the discount.
4. **Check the RAN** (Registro Agrario Nacional) if the land's agrarian status is unclear.
5. **Never pay against a cesión de derechos**, a private contract, or "we'll regularize it later." The [full due-diligence checklist](/en/is-it-safe-to-buy-property-in-tulum) covers the rest of the purchase.
