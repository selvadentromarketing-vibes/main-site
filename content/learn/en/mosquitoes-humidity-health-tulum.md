This is the question buyers ask last and worry about most, usually after a single bad evening on a terrace at dusk. It deserves a straight answer rather than a photo of a hammock.

## How bad are the mosquitoes, really?

Seasonal, and worse in the jungle than in town. In the dry months from roughly December to April, they are a non-issue on most days. Once the rains start, standing water multiplies everywhere and populations climb fast; the worst hours are dawn and dusk, and the worst places are shaded, still, unventilated corners near water.

A jungle lot is honestly more exposed than a paved street in town — more shade, more leaf litter holding water, more still air under canopy. What offsets it is that jungle-side houses are usually designed for it: screened openings, breeze, elevated living areas, and no accumulating water on site. Design decides your experience here far more than location does.

## What mosquito-borne illnesses exist in the region?

Dengue, Zika and chikungunya are all present in southeastern Mexico, transmitted by *Aedes aegypti* — a mosquito that bites during the day, breeds in small containers of clean standing water, and lives in and around houses rather than deep in the wild. Malaria is not a practical concern on this part of the coast.

Saying that plainly is not alarmism. Dengue is the one residents actually talk about, cases concentrate in the rainy season, and most people who live here for years never get it. Municipal and state health authorities run fumigation and larvicide campaigns during the season, and Mexico's Secretaría de Salud publishes current epidemiological bulletins. Check those, and your own country's current travel health guidance, rather than taking a number from any real estate page — including this one, which is why there are none here.

## What actually works against mosquitoes?

1. **Screens on every opening**, properly fitted, plus screened doors. This is the highest-value item by a wide margin and the one most often value-engineered out of a build.
2. **Eliminate standing water weekly.** Plant pots, saucers, buckets, tarps, roof drains, unused pools, construction debris. *Aedes* breeds in a bottle cap of water. This single habit does more than any spray.
3. **Repellent with DEET, picaridin or IR3535** at dawn and dusk, and long sleeves on jungle walks.
4. **Ceiling fans and cross-ventilation.** Mosquitoes are weak fliers; moving air is genuinely effective on a terrace.
5. **Participate in community fumigation** rather than opting out — the season campaigns only work at neighborhood scale.
6. **Mosquito nets for outdoor sleeping** and for kids' rooms in the wettest months.
7. **Landscape with drainage in mind.** Attractive planting that traps water in bromeliads and low hollows costs you evenings.

## How humid is it, and what does that mean for a house?

Humidity from May through October is the part people underestimate. It does not just feel heavy: it drives mold on interior finishes, corrosion on hardware, musty closets, warped joinery, and equipment failure. Every experienced builder on this coast designs against it, and this is where owning land you build yourself becomes a real advantage over inheriting someone else's shortcuts.

What actually works:

- **Cross-ventilation as the primary strategy.** Openings on opposite walls, aligned with the prevailing easterly breeze off the Caribbean. High ceilings and clerestory or vented openings to let hot air out.
- **Deep overhangs and shaded openings.** Shade the glass, not just the room. Overhangs also keep wind-driven rain out of open windows.
- **A/C sized correctly and used on dry mode.** Oversized units cool fast, run short cycles, and never dehumidify — the classic reason a "cold" house still feels clammy.
- **Standalone dehumidifiers** for closets, storage and closed rooms, and a dehumidifier or A/C left running when the house is empty for weeks. Locked-up humid houses are how mold takes hold.
- **Closed-cell insulation choices.** Where insulation is used on roof slabs, closed-cell products resist moisture absorption in a way open-cell products do not.
- **Mold-resistant finishes.** Mineral or anti-fungal paints, sealed concrete, ceramic and stone floors, marine-grade or stainless hardware. Avoid wall-to-wall carpet and untreated MDF entirely.
- **Air gaps behind cabinetry and wardrobes**, and ventilated closet doors instead of sealed boxes.
- **Roof and terrace drainage that actually clears volume** — flat roof decks need real slope and real outlets, not decorative ones.

One structural note specific to low-density communities: where building rules cap lot coverage — 35% in our case, with a two-level plus roof-deck limit — most of your lot stays canopy. Shade lowers surface temperature around the house and makes the ventilation strategy work better. Preserved jungle is a comfort decision as much as an environmental one.

## What should I expect season by season?

| Period | Weather | Mosquitoes | Humidity | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dec–Feb | Coolest, dry, occasional northern fronts | Low | Comfortable | High tourist season; best weather of the year |
| Mar–May | Hot, dry, building strongly toward the rains | Rising late in the period | Climbing from May | Preferred construction window; sargassum can start |
| Jun–Aug | Hot, afternoon storms, first rains | Peak | High | Hurricane season opens; sargassum common |
| Sep–Nov | Wettest, most storm activity | High, easing in November | High into October | Peak hurricane risk; quietest tourist months |

Two practical readings of that table. If you are building, the dry months are when work moves. If you are visiting to decide whether you could live here, come in September rather than February — see the version of Tulum that people complain about, not the one on the postcards.

## What is healthcare access like?

Tulum has private clinics, pharmacies with attached consultation, dentists and general practitioners, and the level of care for routine issues — a stomach bug, a wound, a dengue diagnosis, a broken bone — is generally fine locally. For anything serious, planned surgery, specialists or intensive care, the referral pattern is Playa del Carmen (about 40 minutes) or Cancún (about 90 minutes), where the large private hospitals are.

Private health insurance is the norm for foreign residents, either an international policy or a Mexican one, and it is worth arranging before you need it. Two details people learn the hard way: private hospitals often expect payment or an insurer authorization at admission, and air-ambulance or medical-evacuation coverage is a separate rider worth pricing if you live at distance from a major hospital. Confirm all of it with an insurance broker who works in Mexico — do not take a real estate page's word for coverage.

## The realistic summary

You will get bitten. Some months you will run a dehumidifier. You will drive to Playa or Cancún for a specialist. None of that is a hidden catastrophe, and all of it is what people who live on a Caribbean coast organize their houses around. The buyers who struggle here are the ones who built as if it were a dry climate.
