A cenote is a natural sinkhole that forms when the Yucatán Peninsula's limestone bedrock collapses, exposing the groundwater beneath. Cenotes connect to the largest underground river network on the planet, and the ancient Maya treated them as sacred wells. In Tulum, they are also something else: a defining feature of where — and how — people live.

One disclosure before we go further. We write about cenotes from a particular vantage point: Selvadentro is a private community on Tulum's jungle side with nine natural cenotes inside its boundaries. That shapes how we see them. It also means we have spent years learning what they are, how they behave, and what it takes to keep them intact.

## How does a cenote form?

The Yucatán Peninsula has almost no surface rivers. The ground is porous limestone — karst, in geological terms — so rainwater does not pool or run; it filters straight down through the rock. Over hundreds of thousands of years, that slightly acidic water dissolves the limestone from the inside, carving out an enormous system of flooded caves and tunnels.

Sometimes the roof of one of those caves thins until it can no longer hold its own weight. It collapses, and the aquifer below is suddenly open to the sky. That opening is a cenote.

The scale of what lies underneath is hard to overstate. The peninsula holds the largest underground river network on the planet, and every cenote is a window into it. The water is famously clear because it has been filtered through rock rather than carried across soil — which is also why cenotes are so sensitive to what happens on the surface above them.

## What types of cenotes exist?

Cenotes are usually grouped into three broad types, which loosely track their age. The more the ceiling has collapsed, the older the cenote tends to be.

| Type | What it looks like | What it feels like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open | A full collapse — a pool or small lake open to the sky | Sunlight, jungle at the rim, the classic swimming cenote |
| Semi-open | A partial collapse — part cave roof, part open sky | Shafts of light over dark water; the most photographed kind |
| Cavern | The roof is largely intact; you enter through an opening | Still air, stalactites, water in near-darkness |

No type is better than another. An open cenote invites a long swim; a cavern cenote asks for quiet. Part of what makes living among several of them interesting is that no two behave alike.

## What did cenotes mean to the Maya?

The word itself is Maya: *ts'onot*, meaning a well or cavern with water, which Spanish speakers rendered as *cenote*. In a land without rivers, cenotes were the water supply — settlements grew around them, and access to one could decide where a city stood.

But they were never just infrastructure. In Maya cosmology, cenotes were thresholds — openings between the everyday world and the world below. Archaeologists have recovered offerings of jade, ceramics and gold from cenote floors across the peninsula. These were places you approached with intention.

That history deserves plain respect, not decoration. We do not name marketing campaigns after gods or stage rituals for visitors. The honest way to honor what cenotes meant is simpler: keep the water clean, keep the jungle around them standing, and treat them as the reason the place matters rather than a backdrop.

## What is the difference between visiting a cenote and living beside one?

Most people meet cenotes as attractions, and Tulum has extraordinary ones. Since the Maya Train began service in December 2023 and the Tulum airport opened the same month, they are easier to reach than ever — which also means entry lines, posted hours, lockers and tour groups. A visit is worth it. It is also a transaction: you arrive, you swim, you leave.

## How does a community protect its cenotes?

Protection starts before design. At Selvadentro, specialists mapped the cenotes and the cave systems feeding them first, and the masterplan was drawn around that map — roads, lots and amenities were placed where the jungle and the water allowed, not the other way around.

Then the rules do the daily work:

1. **A jungle buffer.** 65% of the project's jungle stays untouched, permanently. Around each cenote, vegetation stays in place to filter what reaches the water.
2. **Building limits that bind everyone.** Owners build on a maximum of 35% of their lot, up to two levels plus a roof deck. Less impermeable surface means more rain absorbed the way the aquifer expects.
3. **Infrastructure designed for the subsoil.** An underground water network and sustainable energy systems, built so daily life does not leak into the aquifer.

None of this is exotic. It is restraint, written into regulation — the approach that defines [eco-conscious development in Tulum](/en/eco-friendly-developments-tulum) at its best, and the difference between a cenote that stays clear for the next generation and one that does not.
