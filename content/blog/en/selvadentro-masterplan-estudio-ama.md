The Selvadentro masterplan, designed by Estudio AMA, reverses the usual order of development: specialists first mapped the site's nine cenotes and mature jungle, and only then were roads, lots and amenities drawn where that map allowed. The result, a Tulum community that preserves 65% of its jungle, has been covered by architecture press including ArchEyes and Archello.

This is our own story, told in the first person — Selvadentro is our project, and we are not neutral about it. But it is a story with third-party witnesses, which is rare in this market, and the design logic behind it is worth explaining properly: not as marketing language, but as a sequence of concrete decisions you can verify on the ground.

## Why did the design start with a map instead of a plan?

Most developments begin with a layout: how many units fit, where the clubhouse goes, what remains for green area. The land is then adjusted to fit the drawing.

Estudio AMA worked in the opposite direction. Before anything was drawn, specialists mapped what the land already contained — nine natural cenotes, the cave systems that feed them, and the stands of mature jungle worth keeping. That map became the constraint everything else had to respect. Roads bend where the jungle is oldest. Amenities sit where clearings allowed them. Lots occupy the ground the survey said could take them.

The masterplan question changed from "how much fits here?" to "what does this land allow?" — and the honest answer was: less than the market norm, in exchange for something the market cannot copy.

## What is a "habitable sanctuary"?

That is the phrase we use internally for the concept, and it earns its two words. A sanctuary alone is a reserve you visit; habitable alone is any subdivision. The masterplan's bet is that the two can coexist on one property line — that a community can live inside a preserved ecosystem without hollowing it out.

You do not have to take our word for the idea's merit. The Selvadentro masterplan has been featured by name in the international architecture press — ArchEyes, Archello, ADF Web Magazine and Amazing Architecture have all covered Estudio AMA's design and its jungle-first logic. Publications like these review built and planned work on architectural grounds; they are not selling lots. We cite them because a design claim backed only by the seller is just a claim.

## What does the concept mean concretely?

Concepts are cheap; commitments are checkable. These are the commitments:

- **Nine cenotes, preserved.** Mirador, Playa, Piedra, Luz, Azul, Selva, Madera, Vida and Caverna were mapped with specialists and kept as natural spaces for residents — not converted into ticketed attractions. They anchor the plan; everything else was placed around them. More on each at our [cenotes page](/en/cenotes).
- **65% of the jungle, untouched.** Preservation drawn into the plan itself, reinforced lot by lot through building rules — 35% maximum lot coverage, two levels plus roof deck, binding on every owner.
- **Amenities placed by the jungle, not over it.** The 12+ experiences — from the Jungle Gym and wellness center to the Pabellón Holístico, Kids Jungle and the treehouse — occupy the sites the mapping allowed, threaded together by trails rather than imposed on a grid. The full list is on our [amenities page](/en/amenities).
- **Casa de los Cenotes as the heart.** The clubhouse — restaurant, pool bar, the community's gathering point — sits at the center of the plan, so shared life concentrates in one carefully chosen place instead of spreading through the preserved areas.
- **Architecture with a pedigree.** The amenity buildings are by Maat Handasa, the firm that built Chablé Resort — winner at the AHEAD Americas awards in 2017 and a reference for architecture that defers to its landscape.

## What does a resident actually experience at each layer?

A masterplan is abstract until you move through it. This is the sequence, from the outside in:

| Layer | What it is | What you experience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arrival | Controlled access, Avenida Selvadentro | Security at the gate, then a road under canopy — the town noise drops away |
| The enclave | Suspiro's interior street | Low-density lots among trees; no building rises above the jungle line |
| Your lot | 400–1,673 m², 35% buildable | Your home with the majority of your own land as standing jungle |
| The sanctuary | Nine cenotes, trails, lookout | Swimming, walking, silence — resident access, no tickets, no hours |
| The heart | Casa de los Cenotes and amenities | Restaurant, pool bar, wellness, sport, community life in one center |

Two timing facts belong here, stated plainly. Suspiro and its amenities are delivered in 2029 — but residents have access to the cenotes and common areas from the day of purchase, so the sanctuary layer works from day one. And the market has been pricing the plan in: from $119 USD/m² at launch in May 2025 to $167 USD/m² today, with the developer projecting $280–360 USD/m² at close. Our [investment page](/en/investment) shows the full series.

## Why does the masterplan matter to a buyer?

Because in a masterplanned community, you are buying the plan as much as the lot. The plan decides who your neighbors can be, what can never be built beside you, and whether the jungle you saw on the sales visit still exists in fifteen years. A plan drawn around mapped cenotes and enforced by regulation ages differently from one drawn around a sales target.

Selvadentro was designed by Estudio AMA to be judged on exactly that timescale. If you want to judge it yourself, explore the [lots currently available](/en/tulum-land-for-sale) or visit us in Tulum — the masterplan reads best with jungle on both sides of the road.
